How I Rank Prospects
I rank prospects based on how much total aggregate value I believe they can add to a championship-level NBA team.
That means I care most about:
- how much offense a player can create or support 
- playoff translation 
- defensive intelligence and survivability 
- how much a player makes teammates better 
- durability 
- and how easily a player can fit on a great team 
Very few players can drive elite offense with the ball in their hands at the highest level. For everyone else, I care a lot about how much value they can provide without dominating the ball through passing, spacing, cutting, screening, offensive rebounding, avoiding turnovers, quick decision-making, and juicing transition offense.
On defense, I care not just about steals and blocks, but whether a player can execute schemes intelligently, read the game quickly, and survive the brutal isolation and matchup-hunting environment of playoff basketball. I also care about quieter forms of winning value: transition play, defensive rebounding, boxing out, rim protection, switchability, and the ability to wear out opponents.
If a player’s main value comes through scoring, I care a lot about how efficiently he scores and whether that efficiency is likely to survive in the playoffs.
I also care about whether a player’s likely future role and contract are aligned. A player who provides real championship value on a manageable contract can be more useful than a more famous, higher-scoring player whose style is harder to build around and whose eventual salary creates real team-building constraints.
My methodology is straightforward: I use statistics to form hypotheses, stress-test those hypotheses against physical tools and age, then use film, durability, and character research to reach a final conclusion.
This is not a mock draft, and it is not a ranking of who I think will become the biggest star or make the most money. It is my attempt to rank who I believe will provide the most value to high-level NBA winning.
Each prospect entry includes a short explanation of his best path to value, his main concern, and the kind of role I think he can fill on a good team.
My tiers are intentionally strict. History shows that it is a very high bar to become even a good NBA starter, and most drafts do not produce anywhere close to 20 players who reach that level.
*Heights are listed in shoes.
Tier 1 — Superstar
What this tier means: Projects as a top-10 player in the NBA. Has a realistic pathway to becoming the best player on a championship team.
#1 – Cameron Boozer, Power Forward, Duke
Full scout available here: https://www.nba-draft-scouting.com/post/cameron-boozer-scouting-report-nba-draft-projection.
Age: 18.9 Height: 6’9 Wingspan: 7’1.5 Standing Reach: 9’0 Weight: 253 lbs
NBA Strengths: Ultra cerebral and versatile big with excellent strength and coordination. Can dribble drive, pass, space the floor, score in the post, play as a ball handler or screener, screen and cut off ball, roll, offensive rebound, throw outlet passes, defensive rebound, and guard big forwards in isolation. He's the "queen" on the chessboard.
Main concerns: Rim finishing just ok. Lacks elite vertical pop. Struggles to guard quicker players in space.
Role on a good team: Primary initiator from the 4 or offensive centerpiece who can function as either a first or second option.
Why ranked here: Boozer combines elite passing, elite strength, very strong shooting indicators, and rare offensive versatility for a player his age and size. He can create offense for himself and others, scale next to another creator, and generate value as a scorer, passer, screener, rebounder, and spacer. The defense is not perfect, but the offensive package is so complete and so scalable that he projects as the best prospect in the class and a legitimate top-10-player-in-the-NBA outcome.
NBA value comp: Tyrese Haliburton in Paolo Banchero's body (s/o to Parker Fleming). Top 10 player.
Tier 2 — All-Star
What this tier means: Projects as a top-30 player in the NBA. Good enough to be one of the three best players on a championship team, even if not the main engine.
#2 – AJ Dybantsa, Small Forward, BYU
Age: 19.4 Height: 6’9.5” Wingspan: 7’0.5” Standing Reach: 8'10 Weight: 217 lbs
Full scout here: https://www.nba-draft-scouting.com/post/aj-dybantsa-scouting-report-nba-projection.
Best NBA skill: Elite rim pressure as a 6’9.5 wing shot creator. Positive indicators of touch and passing ability. Real shotmaking ability.
Main concern: His scoring profile is still too midrange- and isolation-heavy, and his three-point evolution will likely determine whether he becomes a true offensive star or merely a high-usage scorer. Lacks strength. Doesn't contribute much on defense.
Role on a good team: High-usage scoring wing and secondary offensive engine who is best optimized next to a pass-first initiator.
Why ranked here: Dybantsa is an elite young scorer with rare fluidity, rim pressure, foul-drawing ability, and shot-creation talent for his size. He already generates offense at a very high level, and if his shot selection and three-point volume improve, he has legitimate superstar upside. The reason he is not higher is that his current offensive style leans too heavily on difficult midrange creation, and I do not yet fully trust the offensive scalability or defensive impact at the highest level.
NBA value comp: Jayson Tatum-style offensive impact if the shot profile evolves; DeMar DeRozan outcome risk.
#3 – Caleb Wilson, Power Forward, North Carolina
Full scout here: https://www.nba-draft-scouting.com/post/caleb-wilson-2026-nba-draft-scouting-report-projection
Age: 19.9 Height: 6’10” Wingspan: 7’0” Standing Reach: 9'0 Weight: 211 lbs
Best NBA skill: Elite rim finishing with shotmaking and excellent weakside defensive playmaking from the 4.
Main concern: His path to star offense is narrow because he lacks standout strength and length for the position and is not yet a real three-point shooter or primary creator.
Role on a good team: Third offensive option and major defensive playmaker from the 4.
Why ranked here: Wilson projects as a highly impactful two-way power forward whose value should come through finishing, rebounding, transition, passing, and disruptive help defense rather than primary offensive engine creation. He is an elite vertical athlete, a great event creator on defense, and a very strong rebounder with real feel on both ends. The reason he is not higher is that his physical profile narrows the offensive upside: he is somewhat strength- and length-deficient relative to many star NBA 4s, and his halfcourt creation and shooting still leave real questions. Still, the total package gives him a strong pathway to high-level winning impact that could outstrip his raw star reputation.
NBA value comp: Skinny Bam Adebayo with more shot making.
#4 – Darryn Peterson, Shooting Guard, Kansas
Full scout here: https://www.nba-draft-scouting.com/post/darryn-peterson-scouting-report-nba-projection.
Age: 19.4 Height: 6’5.5” Wingspan: 6’10” Standing Reach: 8'7 Weight: 199 lbs
Best NBA skill: High-level perimeter shotmaking with real off-ball scoring value.
Main concern: He does not generate enough rim pressure or playmaking to project as a true primary offensive engine, and the durability profile introduces real uncertainty.
Role on a good team: High-usage scoring 2 and secondary creator next to a dominant initiator.
Why ranked here: Peterson projects as a high-end scoring guard with real shooting indicators, strong foul-drawing ability, and valuable off-ball movement, which gives him a clean pathway to major offensive value on good teams. He can score efficiently from the perimeter, punish closeouts, and add defensive playmaking on the other end, but he is more Booker/Murray than true heliocentric lead guard because the rim pressure and passing are not at that level. He is ranked this high because high-level shotmaking wings with size, touch, and secondary value are extremely valuable, but not higher because the creation ceiling, durability concerns, and limited primary-engine traits cap the overall upside.
NBA value comp: Devin Booker with more defense.
Tier 3 — Good Starter
What this tier means: Projects as a top-100 player in the NBA. Good enough to start on a championship team and provide clear positive value.
#5 – Yaxel Lendeborg, Power Forward, Michigan
Age: 23.7 Height: 6’10” Wingspan: 7’3” Standing Reach: 9’0.5” Weight: 241 lbs
Best NBA skill: Rare two-way versatility for a big forward who can space the floor, attack in motion, rebound, pass, and defend across multiple matchups.
Main concern: He is older and unlikely to become a true offensive engine, which caps the upside even though the overall game is extremely well-rounded.
Role on a good team: Starting 4 who fits seamlessly with stars and provides positive value on both ends.
Why ranked here: Lendeborg projects as an average-to-above-average NBA starter because he does almost everything well outside of true primary creation: he can space the floor, attack shifting defenses, finish, pass, rebound, run in transition, and defend both in space and around the rim. The impact metrics are historic, the RAPM is elite, and the film supports a very fluid, mobile forward who plays within structure and makes winning plays without needing to dominate the ball. He is not ranked higher because he is already 23.7 and does not project as a go-to scorer, offensive centerpiece, or DPOY. But as an immediate-value, top-50-player type bet who can fit next to stars on a rookie contract, he is immensely appealing.
NBA value comp: The Derrick White of power forwards.
#6 – Brayden Burries, Wing Guard, Arizona
Age: 20.8 Height: 6’5” Wingspan: 6’6” Standing Reach: 8’2.5” Weight: 205 lbs
Best NBA skill: Very good three-level scoring anchored by excellent 3pt shooting and efficient decision-making within the flow of offense.
Main concern: He is older for a freshman, lacks length and elite quickness, and may be physically limited to the 2 without a truly elite athletic or creation advantage.
Role on a good team: Starting or high-end rotation 2 guard who scores efficiently, plays within structure, and holds up on both ends.
Why ranked here: Burries projects as a very clean fit on a good NBA team because he is an efficient scorer, a strong shooting prospect, and a competitive, reasonably physical guard who does not have glaring weaknesses. I do not see star-level self-created dominance here, but I do see a player who can shoot more threes in the NBA, attack closeouts, finish well enough, and provide average-to-above-average value on both ends. He is not ranked higher because he is older, probably limited to the 2, and lacks a single overwhelming elite trait beyond his shooting. Still, sometimes the right draft pick is the layup, and Burries profiles like a very strong bet to help winning.
NBA value comp: Desmond Bane-lite / starting-caliber 2 guard.
#7 – Darius Acuff Jr., Point Guard, Arkansas
Full scout here: https://www.nba-draft-scouting.com/post/darius-acuff-2026-nba-draft-scouting-report-projection.
Age: 19.6 Height: 6’3” Wingspan: 6’6.5” Standing Reach: 8'2.5 Weight: 185.8 lbs
Best NBA skill: Elite pull-up shooting and paint-touch generation as a scoring lead guard.
Main concern: He is a very poor defender and does not provide enough off-ball value to comfortably add value if the on-ball offense falls short of true star level.
Role on a good team: High-usage scoring point guard and primary on-ball creator who needs strong defensive support around him.
Why ranked here: Acuff projects as a highly skilled scoring point guard with near-star-level offensive value, driven by elite shooting indicators, real pull-up gravity, strong burst, and very good pick-and-roll passing. He should be able to run offense, create efficient jump shots, and generate enough paint touches to be a productive NBA starter or even an All-Star level offensive player. He is not ranked higher because the defensive projection is very poor, the off-ball value is limited, and I do not fully buy the offense as dominant enough to erase those weaknesses at the highest level. That leaves him with a narrower championship pathway than his raw offensive talent suggests.
NBA value comp: Kemba Walker.
#8 – Allen Graves, Power Forward, Santa Clara
Age: 19.9 Height: 6’9”Wingspan: 7’0”Standing Reach: 8’10.5”Weight: 225.6 lbs
Best NBA skill: Elite team-defense playmaking built on lightning-quick hands, anticipation, and scheme execution.
Main concern: He is not an explosive finisher or real shot creator, and if the shooting regresses, the offensive ceiling gets shakier.
Role on a good team: Starting 4 who spaces the floor, makes quick reads, crashes the offensive glass, and drives winning with help defense, rebounding and connective play.
Why ranked here: Graves projects as a good starter because he is a super high-IQ forward who fits almost everything good teams want from a complementary 4. Offensively, he can pick-and-pop, keep the ball moving, pass out of short roll, attack the offensive glass at a high level, and avoid turnovers, which is a huge plus for a young forward. Defensively, I buy him as a major positive because the feel is outstanding, the hands are absurdly quick, and he consistently makes plays all over the floor despite lacking standout raw athletic pop. He is not ranked higher because he is not a true creator, the rim finishing is shaky, the shooting still needs a little more proof, and he cannot play the 5 against real NBA size.
NBA value comp: High-IQ starting 4 in a Kyle Anderson / Boris Diaw mold.
#9 – Ebuka Okorie, Point Guard, Stanford
Age: 19.2 Height: 6’2” Wingspan: 6’8” Standing Reach: 8’2” Weight: 186 lbs
Best NBA skill: Relentless rim pressure paired with smart, low-mistake decision-making.
Main concern: His off-ball utility appears limited, and while the pull-up shooting is real enough, he is probably not an elite enough shooter or finisher to become a true star.
Role on a good team: Good starting point guard who attacks the paint, creates kickouts, keeps turnovers low, and can hold up defensively within structure.
Why ranked here: Okorie projects as a good NBA starter because he is extremely effective at getting to the rim, plays with real patience and control, and combines his downhill creation with good passing vision and very low turnovers for a high-usage freshman guard. I buy the offense because the blow-by ability is obvious, the foul drawing is strong, and the pull-up shooting is good enough to keep defenses honest even if it is not elite. He is not ranked higher because the off-ball value is basically nonexistent right now (at least within Stanford's team context), the finishing percentage is a little shakier than the eye test suggests, and I do not think the overall offensive package is quite overwhelming enough to project star-level offense. Defensively, though, the wingspan, quickness, and instincts give him a good chance to be adequate or a little better, which helps support the starter projection.
NBA value comps: Ajay Mitchell.
#10 – Cameron Carr, Shooting Guard, Baylor
Age: 21.6 Height: 6’5.5” Wingspan: 7’1” Standing Reach: 8’8” Weight: 184 lbs
Best NBA skill: High-level off-ball scoring built on three-point volume, movement shooting, closeout attacking, and explosive finishing.
Main concern: He is very skinny, turnover-prone when pushed into on-ball creation, and still too inconsistent defensively to cleanly project as a strong two-way starter.
Role on a good team: Starting shooting guard who spaces the floor, flies off movement, attacks closeouts, and adds some secondary rim protection and rebounding from the 2.
Why ranked here: Carr profiles as a starting-caliber shooting guard because he can score efficiently without monopolizing the ball, which is an extremely valuable skill on good teams. He is a real off-ball threat who can bomb threes, cut for dunks, punish closeouts, and keep the offense flowing without sticky decision-making. He is not ranked higher because I do not buy him as a primary creator: the frame is weak, the handle gets loose, and the self-created offense leans too much on midrange pull-ups and inconsistent decision-making. Defensively, I think the tools are strong enough for neutral value overall, but the projection is still volatile because the engagement and fundamentals need real work. He should be able to guard quick perimeter wings but will struggle against strong wings.
NBA value comps: Hybrid of Jalen Green and Devin Vassell.
#11 – Bennett Stirtz, Point Guard, Iowa
Age: 22.7 Height: 6’3.5” Wingspan: 6’6” Standing Reach: 8’2.5” Weight: 186 lbs
Best NBA skill: Starting-level halfcourt orchestration built on pull-up shooting, smart pick-and-roll play, and relentless advantage creation within structure.
Main concern: He probably does not have the athletic juice to consistently create one-on-one against great playoff defenses, which caps the offensive ceiling. His overall physical tools are also just average for an NBA point guard.
Role on a good team: Starting point guard who thrives next to a dominant scorer, runs offense cleanly, and keeps pressure on defenses with shooting, offensive flow, and decision-making.
Why ranked here: Stirtz projects as a starting-level NBA point guard because he can run a halfcourt offense, bomb pull-up threes, finish efficiently at the rim, make good pick-and-roll reads, and handle huge minute loads without turning the ball over much. The durability, workload tolerance, and winning résumé are all major positives, and I buy the offense as clearly translatable. He is not ranked higher because I do not think he has the athleticism to consistently beat elite switching playoff defenses as a true offensive engine. The best version of him is paired with a star scorer and a smart coach who can let him attack tilted defenses, space the floor, and keep the offense organized. Defensively, he should be "good enough." He's smart, plays hard, and has good lateral agility, although he will be bullied in certain matchups.
NBA value comp: Immanuel Quickley/Ty Jerome.

Tier 4 — Good Rotation Player
What this tier means: Projects as a top-200 player in the NBA. Can meaningfully contribute to a great team and swing certain games. May start or come off the bench depending on team context.
#14 – Morez Johnson Jr., Power Forward, Michigan
Age: 20.4 Height: 6’10” Wingspan: 7’3.5” Standing Reach: 8’11” Weight: 250.6 lbs
Best NBA skill: Elite motor paired with high-level rim finishing, athleticism, screening, and offensive rebounding.
Main concern: His sub-9’0” standing reach and limited skill/passing profile make him an awkward fit who could be played off the floor in certain high-leverage matchups.
Role on a good team: Good rotation big who plays mostly at the 5, finishes plays, attacks the glass, and defends in aggressive coverages.
Why ranked here: Johnson projects as a clear positive NBA player because he has obvious, translatable strengths on both ends: he screens, rolls, finishes, rebounds, plays with nonstop energy, and can defend in space better than most 260lbs bigs. The athleticism is real, the motor is elite, and I buy him as someone who can help very good teams without needing touches. He is not ranked higher because the skill level is still fairly narrow, he is not a real playmaking 4, and the sub-9’0” standing reach limits both his drop coverage value and overall rim protection. That leaves him as a good rotation player rather than a clean starter bet.
NBA value comp: High-motor rotation 5 with some switch/blitz value.
#15 – Zuby Ejiofor, Power Forward, St. John’s
Age: 22.2 Height: 6’8.5” Wingspan: 7’2” Standing Reach: 8’11” Weight: 245.2 lbs
Best NBA skill: Productive interior offense built on foul drawing, offensive rebounding, passing feel, and overall activity, as well as strong offensive rebounding, feel, and competitiveness.
Main concern: His position is hard to solve because he does not finish well enough at the rim for a true 5 or defensive rebound, but he also does not currently shoot well enough from 3 to cleanly play the 4.
Role on a good team: Good rotation big who can play mostly at the 4, attack the glass, move the ball, provide secondary rim protection, and punish teams if the jumper ever becomes credible.
Why ranked here: Ejiofor is a difficult evaluation because so much of the profile is good: he is highly productive, draws fouls at an elite rate, rebounds offensively, passes very well for his size, and I buy him as a real positive defender whether he is playing as a mobile 4 or a backup 5. The problem is that his cleanest offensive role depends on a skill that is not there yet: if he can hit open 3s, he becomes a very valuable modern 4 and a clear good-starter bet, but if he cannot, the fit gets much murkier because I do not want him living full-time at center. He is not ranked higher because the rim finishing is only fine for a big, the defensive rebounding is weak for a 5, and the shooting is the million-dollar swing skill in the whole evaluation. That leaves me most comfortable projecting a good rotation player, even though there is clearly more upside than that if the shot comes around.
NBA value comp: Difficult tweener eval, but a very strong rotation-big (or even good starter) outcome is real if the 3 becomes playable.
#16 – Mikel Brown Jr., Point Guard, Louisville
Age: 20.2 Height: 6’4.5” Wingspan: 6’7.5” Standing Reach: 8'4.5” Weight: 190.2 lbs
Best NBA skill: Dynamic pull-up shooting range that bends defenses and opens up his drives and passing.
Main concern: He may be more of a scoring weapon than a true lead initiator, and the lingering back issue adds real durability uncertainty.
Role on a good team: High-level scoring guard who can captain offense in stretches but is best optimized next to a stronger primary initiator.
Why ranked here: Brown projects as a clear NBA player because he combines plus size for a point guard with very good athleticism, deep pull-up shooting range, good finishing craft, and real passing vision. He can absolutely punish shifting defenses, create offense in pick-and-roll and DHO contexts, and change games with his scoring in a CJ McCollum / Anfernee Simons mold. He is not ranked higher because I’m skeptical of the true star-lead-guard pathway: he can be turnover-prone, seems more instinctual than fully organized as an offensive conductor, may wear down over full games, and the recurring back issue is a real concern. Still, the offensive talent is strong enough that I buy him as a very effective NBA guard.
NBA value comp: CJ McCollum / Anfernee Simons.
#17 – Aday Mara, Center, Michigan
Age: 21.2 Height: 7’4” Wingspan: 7’6” Standing Reach: 9’9” Weight: 260 lbs
Best NBA skills: Elite rim protection and rim finishing.
Main concern: His durability/load tolerance and playoff survivability are major concerns because he gets winded quickly, has never played even 60% of available college minutes, and can be stressed by space.
Role on a good team: Drop-coverage center who protects the rim, finishes plays as a screener/roller, and gives a team 20 or so strong minutes.
Why ranked here: Mara’s translation case is real because the two most important strengths in his profile — rim deterrence and rim finishing — are both extremely strong and extremely NBA-relevant. He should provide positive regular-season value as a big roll man, elite interior finisher, and high-IQ halfcourt defender who can execute more coverages than most giant centers. He is not ranked higher because the overall utility looks capped: he is very limited offensively outside the paint, I do not buy the passing as a major NBA weapon, certain playoff matchups will target and shrink his value, and there is no good reason to project high-minute durability for a 7’4 center who has never cleared 60% of college minutes. That leaves him as a good rotation player rather than a starter-level bet.
NBA value comp: Ryan Kalkbrenner with better rim protection and defensive mobility.
#18 – Bruce Thornton, PG, Ohio State
Age: 22.8 Height: 6’1” Wingspan: 6’5” Standing Reach: 8’0.5” Weight: 224 lbs (approximation based off competing measurements)
Best NBA skill: Extremely efficient three-level scoring driven by elite strength, balance, touch, and very low turnover offense.
Main concern: He is undersized and a limited athlete, and he looks more like a scoring combo guard than a true primary NBA point guard.
Role on a good team: Good rotation guard who scores efficiently on or off the ball, attacks closeouts, and fits best next to a bigger primary creator.
Why ranked here: Thornton has a real NBA value path because he is a unique offensive player: he scores efficiently from all three levels, almost never turns it over, plays well off the ball, and uses overwhelming strength and control to compensate for ordinary burst and poor vertical pop. I buy the scoring because it is not built on fake college-only tricks; the touch, balance, floater game, off-ball movement, and power all look real. He is not ranked higher because I do not trust him as a full-time lead guard, and the physical limitations likely cap both the defensive ceiling and the level of offensive creation he can provide against elite NBA athletes. That leaves me most comfortable projecting a good rotation player, even though there is a real good-starter pathway in the right system. The NBA is also lower on him than me, so it's hard to predict whether he will get a fair chance at the league.
NBA value comp: Fred VanVleet-style player with less defensive and offensive playmaking and more bully-ball scoring.
#19 – Labaron Philon, Point Guard, Alabama
Age: 20.6 Height: 6’3.5” Wingspan: 6’6” Standing Reach: 8’3.5” Weight: 176 lbs
Best NBA skill: Shifty pick-and-roll creation paired with real passing feel and high-level three-point shotmaking.
Main concern: He is extremely light for an NBA lead guard and not exceptionally athletic and I do not see the best-in-the-world touch and skill for a player with such physical limitations to become a star.
Role on a good team: Good rotation guard off the bench who can lead offense for stretches and juice second units.
Why ranked here: Philon is a very skilled offensive guard who can run pick-and-roll, create pull-up threes, get to the rim with shiftiness and body control, and set up teammates at a high level. The offensive production is real: he captained a great Alabama offense, improved meaningfully as a passer, and has the craft to create shots without elite burst. He is not ranked higher because the frame is a major concern, the athleticism is good but not special, and I project him as a clear NBA defensive negative. That leaves me more comfortable projecting a high-end bench creator than a true star-level lead guard.

#20 – Jayden Quaintance, Center, Kentucky
Age: 19.0 Height: 6’10” Wingspan: 7’5” Standing Reach: 9’1” Weight: 253.4 lbs
Best NBA skill: High-end defensive upside built on elite shot-blocking indicators, real mobility in space, and the tools to play multiple coverages.
Main concern: His offense was poor as a freshman and the ACL plus lingering knee swelling materially lowers confidence in the projection.
Role on a good team: Good rotation center who protects the rim, defends in space, finishes plays, and provides value as a roll man and offensive rebounder.

He is not ranked higher because the injury risk is serious, the lost development year matters, and I do not think the offensive game is remotely polished enough right now to support a more optimistic overall projection. That leaves me projecting a good rotation player with major defensive talent rather than a clean starter bet.
NBA value comp: Young Robert Williams III-type rotation big with more uncertainty.
#21 – Hannes Steinbach, Big, Washington
Age: 20.1 Height: 6’11” Wingspan: 7’2” Standing Reach: 9’0” Weight: 248 lbs
Best NBA skill: Elite rebounding paired with unusual fluidity, coordination, and offensive utility for a big.
Main concern: He is caught between positions: not enough rim protection to cleanly anchor the 5, and not yet enough shooting/playmaking certainty to cleanly start at the 4.
Role on a good team: Good rotation big who can play either frontcourt spot, crush the glass, run in transition, screen, and provide some surprise offensive skill.
Why ranked here: Steinbach is a very difficult scout because the productivity is real, the rebounding is elite, and the film shows more fluidity, perimeter comfort, and offensive versatility than a normal big his size. There are plausible starter pathways here — especially if the corner three becomes real enough for him to play more 4 on offense — but I think those paths are still too attenuated to bet on heavily. He is not a clean 5 because the rim protection is underwhelming, and not a clean 4 because the shooting and perimeter skill are still more theoretical than proven. I’m most comfortable projecting him as a good rotation player who can help in a lot of ways without cleanly solving a position.
NBA value comp: Rotation big with some hybrid 4/5 utility.
#22 – Dailyn Swain, Small Forward, Texas
Age: 20.9 Height: 6’7.5” Wingspan: 6’10” Standing Reach: 8’8.5” Weight: 211.2 lbs
Best NBA skill: Downhill offensive creation from the wing, especially getting to the rim, drawing fouls, and making plays on the move.
